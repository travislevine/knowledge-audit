The business of software, and review

Software is built by teams, for a business, under constraints of time, money, risk and law. This last week covers the side of the job degrees often skip: how teams plan and ship, how reliability and technical debt become business decisions, how engineering is measured and what it costs, and the privacy and licensing rules you'll work within. Lesson 10.1 · 40 min

How software teams work

Who does what

RoleOwns
Product managerWhat to build and why: customer problems, priorities, the roadmap, and measuring whether features worked
Engineering managerThe team's people, hiring, growth, delivery and health
Tech lead / staff engineerHow it's built: technical direction, design reviews, quality, unblocking others
DesignerUser experience: research, flows, interfaces
QA / test engineerTest strategy, automation, exploratory testing
SRE / platform engineerReliability, infrastructure, deployment tooling and on-call practices

As an engineer you'll work with all of them. The skills that matter: understanding the problem behind a request, explaining technical trade-offs in business terms ("this takes two weeks longer but halves our cloud bill"), and raising risks early.

Ways of working

Agile is a set of values: deliver working software in small increments, get feedback early, and adapt the plan rather than following it blindly. The two common frameworks:

  • Scrum works in fixed sprints (usually two weeks): sprint planning, a short daily stand-up, a review that demos the work to stakeholders, and a retrospective on how the team can improve.
  • Kanban is continuous flow: work moves across a board (to do, in progress, review, done) with limits on work in progress. Finishing work beats starting more work; too many parallel tasks slows everything down.

Before building anything significant, teams usually write a design document (or RFC) describing the problem, options considered, the chosen approach and its risks, and ask for review. A day of writing can save weeks of building the wrong thing.

Estimation

Software estimates are notoriously optimistic: the planning fallacy, compounded by unknowns you can't see until you start. Better habits: break work into small pieces, estimate with ranges ("3 to 6 days") rather than single numbers, compare with how long similar work actually took, flag the riskiest unknowns and do them first, and update estimates as you learn. Story points measure a team's relative effort; they're useful for that team's planning, and meaningless for comparing teams.

A product manager asks for "a dashboard showing all user data". What's the best first response from an engineer?

  1. Start building it immediately
  2. Ask what decision or problem the dashboard is for and who will use it, so you build the smallest thing that solves the real need
  3. Refuse, because dashboards are a waste of time
  4. Estimate it at two weeks without asking anything
Requests often describe a solution. Understanding the underlying problem frequently reveals a much simpler fix, or one that matters more.

A Kanban board shows each engineer working on four tasks at once, and nothing is finishing. What's the standard fix?

  1. Add more columns to the board
  2. Hire more engineers
  3. Switch to Scrum
  4. Set work-in-progress limits so people finish and ship work before starting new tasks
Context switching and half-finished work deliver no value. Limiting work in progress shortens the time from starting a task to shipping it.

Lesson 10.2 · 45 min

Reliability as a business decision

100% reliability is impossible and, approaching it, ruinously expensive. Users can't tell 99.99% from 100% (their own Wi-Fi is less reliable than that), and every extra nine slows feature development. So reliability is a target chosen by the business, not an absolute.

SLIs, SLOs and SLAs

  • A service level indicator (SLI) is a measurement of what users experience: the fraction of requests that succeed, or the fraction served in under 300 ms.
  • A service level objective (SLO) is the internal target for it: "99.9% of requests succeed over a rolling 30 days."
  • A service level agreement (SLA) is a contract with customers, with consequences (usually refunds or credits) if it's missed. It's set looser than the SLO, so you notice problems before they cost money.

Error budgets

An SLO of 99.9% means 0.1% of requests may fail: that's the error budget. Over 30 days, it's about 43 minutes of full downtime. The budget turns a vague argument ("ship faster" versus "be more careful") into a shared rule: while there's budget left, ship; when it's spent, slow down and invest in reliability until it recovers.

Incidents and on-call

Building on week 8: during an incident, an incident commander coordinates, someone handles communication (status page, stakeholders), and others investigate. Mitigate first. Afterwards, the blameless postmortem records the timeline, impact, contributing causes and action items, and the action items actually get prioritized. A healthy on-call rotation shares the load, pages only for actionable user-facing problems, and treats a noisy pager as a bug to fix.

A service has a 99.9% availability SLO. Halfway through the month, an outage has used 90% of the error budget. What does an error budget policy typically say?

  1. Slow down or pause risky releases and prioritize reliability work until the budget recovers
  2. Keep shipping as usual, since the budget resets monthly
  3. Lower the SLO to 99%
  4. Stop all deploys permanently
The budget is a shared, agreed lever between product and engineering. Quietly lowering the SLO defeats its purpose.

Why is a company's SLA (the contract) usually looser than its internal SLO?

  1. SLAs are only for marketing
  2. Customers prefer lower numbers
  3. So the team is alerted and can react when the service drops below the internal target, well before it breaches the contract and triggers penalties
  4. SLOs are measured differently
The gap is a safety margin.

Lesson 10.3 · 45 min

Technical debt and architecture decisions

Technical debt

Technical debt is the future cost of shortcuts and outdated designs: every change in that area takes longer (the "interest"), until someone pays it down. Martin Fowler's quadrant distinguishes debt that's deliberate or inadvertent, and prudent or reckless. "We'll ship with a simpler design to hit the launch, and rework it next quarter" is deliberate and prudent: a sensible business trade-off. "What's layering?" is reckless and inadvertent. Some debt is simply what you learn later: "now we know how this should have been designed."

Managing it in practice: make debt visible (write it down with its cost, e.g. "this module causes a third of our incidents"); pay it down continuously, by leaving code a bit better than you found it and reserving some capacity each cycle; and prioritize debt in the code you change most often, since debt in code nobody touches costs little.

Refactor or rewrite?

Big-bang rewrites are famously risky: the old system keeps changing, it encodes years of edge cases nobody documented, and you deliver nothing until the end. The safer approach is the strangler fig: put the new system beside the old one, route one feature at a time to it, and retire the old system piece by piece.

Build or buy?

Build what differentiates your product. Buy (or use open source for) what doesn't, such as authentication, payments, email delivery or monitoring, unless scale, cost or control clearly argue otherwise. Compare the total cost of ownership, not just the price tag:

Architecture and organizations

Conway's law: organizations build systems that mirror their communication structure. Three teams building a compiler tend to produce a three-pass compiler. So team boundaries and service boundaries should be designed together. Microservices let independent teams deploy independently, at a real cost in operational complexity, network failures and distributed data. A well-structured modular monolith is usually the better start for a small team; split out services when team or scaling needs justify it.

Record significant decisions in architecture decision records (ADRs): short documents with the context, the decision, the alternatives considered and the consequences. They answer the question every new team member eventually asks: "why on earth did we do it this way?"

A team wants to rewrite a 10-year-old billing system from scratch over 18 months. What's usually the safer approach?

  1. Rewrite it from scratch, but faster
  2. The strangler fig: build new parts alongside the old system, route features over one at a time, and retire the old code gradually while delivering value throughout
  3. Leave it untouched forever
  4. Rewrite it in a new language without tests
Incremental replacement keeps the system working, reduces risk, and lets you stop at any point with value delivered.

According to Conway's law, what happens if a company organizes engineering into separate frontend, backend and database teams?

  1. Each team will build better software
  2. The architecture won't be affected
  3. The teams will merge into one
  4. The system will tend to split along those lines, with every feature needing coordination between all three teams
This is why many companies organize teams around business areas (search, checkout), each owning its full stack: the architecture follows the team structure.

Lesson 10.4 · 40 min

Measuring engineering and the cost of software

DORA metrics

Years of research by the DORA team (published in the book Accelerate and the annual State of DevOps reports) found four metrics that predict software delivery performance:

MetricMeasuresKind
Deployment frequencyHow often you release to productionSpeed
Lead time for changesTime from commit to running in productionSpeed
Change failure rateShare of deploys that cause a failure in productionStability
Time to restore serviceHow quickly you recover from a failureStability

The key finding: speed and stability aren't a trade-off. The best teams are better at all four at once, because small, frequent, automated releases (week 8) are both faster and safer.

Metrics that mislead

Goodhart's law: when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure. Lines of code rewards verbosity. Story points per sprint inflate. Number of bugs closed rewards splitting bugs. Test coverage targets produce tests that check nothing (week 8). Use metrics to learn about a system or team, combine speed with quality measures, and never rank individuals by output counts. The metrics that matter most to the business are about outcomes: did the feature increase activation, retention or revenue, or reduce support tickets?

What software costs

Engineers' time is usually the biggest cost, which is why productivity and build-versus-buy decisions matter so much. Cloud bills are next. The main drivers:

  • Compute: servers, containers and functions, often over-provisioned "just in case". Rightsizing and autoscaling help, and committed-use discounts suit steady load.
  • Storage: cheap per gigabyte, but it grows forever unless you set retention policies (old logs, backups, abandoned buckets).
  • Data transfer (egress): moving data out of a cloud, or between regions, is charged and easy to overlook.
  • Managed services: convenient, sometimes priced per request in ways that surprise you at scale.
  • Logging and observability: verbose logs at high traffic can cost more than the servers producing them.

Track unit costs, such as cost per thousand requests or cost per active customer, rather than just the total. A growing bill is fine if each customer costs less to serve. Making cost visible to the teams that create it is called FinOps.

A manager proposes ranking engineers by lines of code written per month. What's the main problem?

  1. Lines of code are hard to count
  2. It's fair, but should be weekly
  3. Goodhart's law: people will optimize the number (verbose code, avoiding deletions), while the best work often removes code or prevents problems; output counts don't measure value
  4. It only works in Python
Deleting 2,000 lines of dead code is often more valuable than adding 2,000. Measure outcomes and team-level delivery (like DORA), not individual output.

Which pair of DORA metrics measures stability rather than speed?

  1. Change failure rate, and time to restore service
  2. Deployment frequency, and lead time for changes
  3. Lines of code, and story points
  4. Uptime, and test coverage
Two speed metrics plus two stability metrics stop a team from "improving" by trading one for the other.

Monthly cloud spend grew 40% while customers grew 60%. Is that a problem?

  1. Yes, because any cost growth is bad
  2. Probably not: cost per customer fell, so the service is getting more efficient as it grows
  3. Yes, because cloud costs should be fixed
  4. It's impossible to tell anything from this
Unit costs (per customer, per request) say more about efficiency than total spend.

Lesson 10.5 · 35 min

Privacy, compliance and licensing

Personal data and privacy law

Privacy laws such as the EU's GDPR, Australia's Privacy Act 1988 (with its Australian Privacy Principles and the Notifiable Data Breaches scheme) and California's CCPA apply to almost any software that handles personal data: anything that identifies a person, directly or in combination (names, emails, IP addresses, location, device IDs). The principles that most affect engineering:

  • Data minimization: collect only what you need, and keep it only as long as you need it. Data you don't store can't leak.
  • Purpose limitation and lawful basis: use data only for the reasons you collected it for, with consent or another legal basis.
  • Individuals' rights: people can ask to see, correct or delete their data. Your system must be able to find all of a person's data, including in logs, backups, analytics and third-party tools.
  • Security and breach notification: protect personal data appropriately, and notify regulators (within 72 hours under GDPR) and affected people when a serious breach occurs.
  • Privacy by design: build these in from the start. Don't log personal data unnecessarily, pseudonymize where possible, and control who can access what.

Penalties are serious (GDPR fines reach 4% of global annual turnover), but the bigger cost of a breach is usually lost trust.

Compliance standards

Businesses selling to other businesses are often asked to prove their security practices: SOC 2 and ISO 27001 audit controls like access management, change management, logging and incident response. Taking card payments brings PCI DSS, which is why most companies let a payment provider like Stripe handle card numbers entirely. For engineers this shows up as: changes go through review, access is granted by role and removed promptly, production actions are logged, and secrets are managed properly. These are mostly good engineering practices with paperwork attached.

Open-source licenses

Almost all software depends on open source, and every package comes with a license that says what you may do with it:

TypeExamplesIn short
PermissiveMIT, BSD, Apache 2.0Use it anywhere, including in closed-source products; keep the copyright notice. Apache 2.0 also includes an explicit patent grant.
CopyleftGPLIf you distribute software that includes GPL code, you must release your combined source under the GPL too.
Network copyleftAGPLLike the GPL, but also triggered by letting users interact with the software over a network, as with a web service.
No license(public code with no license file)All rights reserved by default: you may not legally reuse it.

Most companies keep a list of approved licenses and scan dependencies automatically. Check before adding a dependency, and remember that code you write for an employer usually belongs to the employer.

A user asks you to delete all their personal data. Which places are easiest to forget?

  1. The main users table
  2. The user's profile picture
  3. Their password hash
  4. Application logs, analytics tools, backups, caches, search indexes and third-party services the data was sent to
Personal data spreads. Data minimization, keeping it out of logs, and knowing where it flows make deletion requests (and breaches) far easier to handle.

Your company builds a closed-source SaaS web app. A developer wants to include an AGPL-licensed library in the server code. Why would legal want to review that first?

  1. AGPL libraries are always insecure
  2. AGPL code can't be used commercially at all
  3. The AGPL's network clause can require you to offer your own source code to users who interact with the service over a network
  4. AGPL only works on Linux
Ordinary GPL obligations are triggered by distributing software; the AGPL extends them to network use, which matters for web services. Permissive licenses like MIT or Apache 2.0 avoid the issue.

The ten weeks in ten lines

WeekThe idea to keep
1Everything is bits; encodings are agreements, and each has edges (overflow, rounding, UTF-8).
2Choose data structures by the operations you need; Big-O tells you how work grows.
3Sort and search to exploit order; model connections as graphs; cache overlapping subproblems.
4The kernel shares the machine; shared memory needs locks; lock in a consistent order.
5Layers wrap each other; TCP is a reliable byte stream, not messages; debug up the layers.
6Never treat input as code or authority; use the right crypto tool; TLS secures the connection, not the app.
7Model data with constraints; index for your queries; transactions and atomic updates prevent lost updates.
8Small changes, tested and released often, with observability and fast rollback.
9Stateless scale-out, caches and queues; assume failure: timeouts, backoff, idempotency.

A page load takes 3 seconds for first-time visitors in another country, but 300 ms for repeat visitors. Based on the trace, what's the most likely reason?

  1. The database is missing an index
  2. First visits pay for DNS, TCP and TLS round trips, each long over intercontinental distances; repeat visits reuse cached DNS and open connections. A CDN closer to users would help most
  3. The CPU is too slow
  4. Repeat visitors have faster computers
Round trips times distance dominate first loads. CDNs, connection reuse and HTTP/3's faster handshake target exactly this.

A checkout request intermittently charges customers twice. Which ideas from this syllabus are most relevant to the fix?

  1. Idempotency keys for retried requests (week 9), and atomic or transactional writes so a retry can't apply twice (week 7)
  2. Binary search and sorting (week 3)
  3. UTF-8 encoding (week 1)
  4. Subnet masks (week 5)
Double effects come from retries of operations with unknown outcomes. Make the operation idempotent, and make the database write atomic.

After a deploy, the error rate jumps from 0.1% to 5%. Combining weeks 8, 9 and 10, what's the best sequence?

  1. Debug in production until you find the bug, then fix forward
  2. Wait to see whether it settles down
  3. Roll back (or turn off the feature flag) to restore service, communicate status, then investigate with logs and traces and write a blameless postmortem; the incident also spends error budget, which may slow further releases
  4. Lower the SLO
Mitigate first, understand second, and learn third. The error budget makes the reliability cost visible to the whole team.
Week 10 in one paragraph

Software is a team sport in service of a business: understand the problem before the solution, work in small increments, and limit work in progress. Reliability targets are business choices expressed as SLOs, and error budgets balance them against speed. Technical debt, rewrites, build-versus-buy and architecture are trade-offs to make explicitly and record. Measure delivery with DORA, beware Goodhart's law, and track unit costs. Respect privacy by design, know your compliance duties, and check licenses. And when a page loads, every layer from bits to business is involved at once.
