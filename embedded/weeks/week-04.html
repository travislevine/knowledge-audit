Lesson 4.1 · 45 min

Control loops

A closed-loop controller measures something (the process variable), compares it to a target (the setpoint) and adjusts an actuator to reduce the error. A thermostat, cruise control and your watering system are all closed loops.

On/off control with hysteresis

The simplest controller turns the actuator fully on or off. With one threshold, noise makes it chatter: on, off, on, off, many times a second near the threshold, wearing out relays and pumps. Hysteresis uses two thresholds: start watering below 30%, stop only above 45%. Between them, keep doing whatever you were doing.

Soil also has delay: water takes minutes to reach the sensor. If you water until the sensor reads 45%, you'll overshoot badly. Better: water in short bursts, then wait (soak) before measuring again. That's the WATERING and SOAKING states from week 1.

PID control

When the actuator can be varied (motor speed, heater power), a PID controller sets its output from three terms:

  • Proportional: output proportional to the error now. Larger gain responds faster, but too large oscillates. P alone usually leaves a small steady error.
  • Integral: accumulates past error, removing the steady error. Too much causes overshoot; clamp it to avoid integral windup when the actuator is saturated.
  • Derivative: reacts to how fast the error is changing, damping overshoot. Sensitive to noise, so filter first.

Tuning is usually empirical: raise P until it oscillates, back off, add I to remove offset, add a little D if it overshoots.

A pump turns on at 30% moisture and off at 30% moisture. Noisy readings hover around 30%. What happens, and what's the fix?

  1. Increase the sampling rate
  2. Add a PID controller
  3. The pump chatters on and off rapidly; use hysteresis (on below 30%, off above 45%)
  4. Nothing
Two thresholds create a dead band where the state is held.

A P-only controller holds a heater 2 °C below its setpoint indefinitely. Which term fixes that?

  1. Increase D
  2. Add I (integral), which accumulates the persistent error
  3. Remove P
  4. Add hysteresis
Steady-state error is what the integral term removes.

Lesson 4.2 · 40 min

Power and batteries

Battery life is a power budget. Average current is the time-weighted average of each mode:

ESP32: awake with Wi-Fi ≈ 120 mA for 5 s, then deep sleep ≈ 0.01 mA for 595 s (every 10 min)
average ≈ (120 × 5 + 0.01 × 595) / 600 ≈ 1.01 mA
2000 mAh battery → 2000 / 1.01 ≈ 1980 hours ≈ 82 days

Without sleep (always awake at about 80 mA), the same battery lasts about a day. Lessons:

  • Sleep is everything. In deep sleep an ESP32 draws microamps; it wakes on a timer (machine.deepsleep(600_000)) or a pin, and restarts from the top of main.py, so state must be saved in RTC memory or flash.
  • Radio time dominates. Connecting to Wi-Fi takes seconds; send batched readings, use static IPs, or use a low-power radio.
  • Hidden drains: development boards have power LEDs and USB-serial chips that draw milliamps permanently; sensors left powered draw current too. Measure with a multimeter in series.
  • Real capacity is less than the label, and falls with cold and age. Budget 70–80%.

Solar plus a small battery makes an outdoor plant monitor maintenance-free, if the average consumption is below what the panel provides on a cloudy week.

A device draws 50 mA for 2 seconds every minute and 0.1 mA the rest of the time. What's its average current?

  1. About 1.8 mA
  2. About 0.1 mA
  3. 50 mA
  4. 25 mA
(50 × 2 + 0.1 × 58) / 60 ≈ 1.76 mA.

An ESP32 in deep sleep should draw microamps, but your board draws 8 mA asleep. What's a likely cause?

  1. The battery is faulty
  2. The development board's power LED, voltage regulator or USB-serial chip draws current regardless of the ESP32
  3. Wi-Fi is still on
  4. The firmware isn't sleeping
Dev boards are made for convenience, not low power. Bare modules or low-power boards fix it.
Lesson 4.3 · 40 min

Reliability and fail-safes

An unattended device must keep working, or fail harmlessly, without anyone watching. Techniques:

  • Watchdog timer: a hardware timer that resets the chip unless the firmware "feeds" it regularly (wdt = WDT(timeout=8000), then wdt.feed() in the main loop). If the code hangs, the device reboots instead of staying stuck. Feed it only when the loop is genuinely healthy, not from a timer interrupt.
  • Fail-safe defaults: on boot, after a reset, or on any error, outputs go to the safe state (pump off). Relays and MOSFETs should be wired so that a dead or resetting MCU means "off".
  • Hard limits independent of logic: a maximum pump run time and a minimum gap between runs, enforced even if the sensor says the soil is dry. A dislodged sensor sitting in dry air would otherwise water forever.
  • Validate inputs: treat impossible readings (0, maximum, or a sudden jump) as faults.
  • Timeouts on everything that waits: network connections, sensor reads, MQTT publishes.
  • Brownout detection and decoupling: capacitors near the board and a supply sized for the load.
  • Observability: publish uptime, reset reason (machine.reset_cause()), free memory and last error, so you can see problems from the dashboard.

Your soil sensor falls out of the pot, so it reads bone dry forever. What protects the plant and your floor?

  1. A maximum run time per watering, a minimum interval between waterings, and a daily maximum, enforced regardless of the sensor
  2. The watchdog timer
  3. Hysteresis
  4. QoS 1
Independent limits stop a wrong input from causing unlimited action.

Where should a watchdog be fed?

  1. Never: it feeds itself
  2. Once at boot
  3. In a timer interrupt every second
  4. In the main loop, after it has completed its work successfully
Feeding from an interrupt would keep it happy even if the main loop hung, defeating the point.
Lesson 4.4 · 35 min

Testing and the road to a product

Hardware is slow to test: you have to wire things, wait for soil to dry, and you can't easily create a fault. So separate logic from hardware access:

  • Put decisions (state machines, filters, control) in plain functions and classes that take readings and time as arguments and return actions. That's how all of this course's mini-projects work, and why they have tests.
  • Keep a thin hardware layer that reads pins and drives outputs. Inject a fake clock and fake sensors in tests (as in CS weeks 8–10).
  • Simulate what you can't easily cause: a sensor fault, a dropped network, a 49-day timer wrap.
  • Then test on real hardware for the rest: hardware-in-the-loop rigs automate that in industry. Simulators like Wokwi run Arduino and ESP32 code in a browser.

From prototype to product

A breadboard prototype becomes a product through: a custom PCB (designed in KiCad), an enclosure rated for the environment (IP65 for outdoors), power design, regulatory certification for radios (RCM in Australia, FCC in the US, CE in Europe), manufacturing tests, a secure provisioning and update process, and support for devices in the field for years. The bill of materials cost is usually a fraction of the final price.

Why write the watering decision logic as a function like decide(moisture, now) that returns an action, instead of reading pins inside it?

  1. It's shorter
  2. It can be tested on a laptop with any readings and times, including faults that are hard to create physically
  3. It uses less power
  4. MicroPython requires it
Separating logic from hardware makes firmware testable.

What's a major step between a working breadboard prototype and a sellable wireless product?

  1. Adding more sensors
  2. Rewriting it in assembly
  3. Switching to a Raspberry Pi
  4. Regulatory certification for the radio, plus enclosure, PCB, manufacturing tests and update support
Certification and production engineering often take longer than the prototype.
