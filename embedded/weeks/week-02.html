Sensors, actuators and buses

Sensors turn the physical world into voltages and bytes, and actuators turn bytes back into motion, light and water. This week covers how analog readings work (including your soil moisture sensor), how PWM controls power, the three serial buses every embedded engineer uses, and how to filter noisy data. It ends with your first larger project: an ESP32 plant monitor.

By the end of this week you can

  • Convert ADC readings to voltages and calibrated physical units
  • Use PWM to control brightness, speed and servos, and switch loads with transistors and relays
  • Compare UART, I2C and SPI, and read a sensor's datasheet and registers
  • Filter noisy readings with median and exponential moving average filters

Lesson 2.1 · 40 min

Analog sensors and the ADC

Many sensors output a voltage that varies with what they measure. An analog-to-digital converter (ADC) turns it into a number. An n-bit ADC divides the range 0 to its reference voltage into 2ⁿ steps:

voltage = raw / (2**bits - 1) × vref
Arduino Uno: 10-bit, 5 V   → 0–1023, about 4.9 mV per step
ESP32:       12-bit, ~3.3 V → 0–4095, about 0.8 mV per step

The number is only as good as the reference and the ADC. The ESP32's ADC is noticeably non-linear near the ends of its range, needs its attenuation set (adc.atten(ADC.ATTN_11DB)) to read up to about 3.3 V, and its ADC2 pins don't work while Wi-Fi is on. So use ADC1 pins (GPIO 32–39) for sensors.

Your soil moisture sensor

Resistive sensors (two bare metal prongs) measure conductivity, and corrode within weeks because current flows through wet soil. Capacitive sensors (a black board with no exposed metal) measure the soil's dielectric and last much longer. Either way, the raw number means nothing until you calibrate: record the reading in air (dry) and in a glass of water (wet), then map linearly:

percent = (dry - raw) / (dry - wet) × 100   # clamp to 0–100

For capacitive sensors the reading falls as moisture rises, which the formula handles. Calibrate each sensor: two of the same model can differ by hundreds of counts. A tip that extends sensor life: power it from a GPIO pin only while reading.

A 12-bit ESP32 ADC with a 3.3 V range reads 2048. Roughly what voltage is that?

  1. 3.3 V
  2. 2.048 V
  3. 0.8 V
  4. 1.65 V
2048 / 4095 × 3.3 ≈ 1.65 V, about half the range.

Your capacitive sensor reads 3000 in air and 1300 in water. What moisture percentage is a reading of 2150?

  1. 50%
  2. 25%
  3. 72%
  4. 2150 is out of range
(3000 − 2150) / (3000 − 1300) = 850 / 1700 = 50%.
Lesson 2.2 · 40 min

PWM and driving actuators

Most MCUs can't output an arbitrary voltage, but they can switch a pin on and off very fast. Pulse-width modulation (PWM) varies the duty cycle, the fraction of each period the pin is high. To a slow device (an LED seen by your eye, a motor with inertia) it behaves like an average voltage of duty × V.

PWM is used for LED brightness, motor speed and servos, which read the pulse width (1–2 ms every 20 ms) as an angle. In MicroPython: PWM(Pin(2), freq=1000, duty_u16=32768) gives 50%.

Switching real loads

  • A logic-level N-channel MOSFET (for example an IRLZ44N) on the low side switches DC loads like pumps and LED strips, and can be PWM'd for speed control.
  • A relay module switches anything, including mains (don't, unless you're qualified), but it's slow, clicks and can't be PWM'd.
  • Motors and relay coils are inductive: when switched off they produce a voltage spike. A flyback diode across the load (most relay modules include one) protects your transistor.
  • Give motors and pumps their own power supply, sharing only ground with the board. A pump starting can sag a shared supply enough to reset the MCU (a brownout).

A 5 V PWM signal at 25% duty cycle drives a motor. What average voltage does the motor effectively see?

  1. 3.75 V
  2. 0.25 V
  3. 5 V
  4. 1.25 V
Average = duty × V = 0.25 × 5 = 1.25 V.

Your ESP32 resets every time the pump turns on. What's the most likely cause?

  1. Wi-Fi interference
  2. A software bug
  3. The PWM frequency is too low
  4. The pump's startup current sags the shared supply and the ESP32 browns out; power the pump separately and share only ground
Motors draw a large inrush current. Separate supplies and decoupling capacitors fix it.
Lesson 2.3 · 45 min

UART, I2C and SPI

Digital sensors and chips talk over serial buses. You'll meet three constantly:

UARTI2CSPI
WiresTX, RX (+ ground)SDA, SCL (+ ground)SCK, MOSI, MISO, plus one CS per device
DevicesTwo (point to point)Many, by 7-bit addressMany, by chip-select pin
SpeedTypically 9600–115200 baud100 kHz–1 MHzUp to tens of MHz
ClockNone: both sides agree on baud rateShared, from controllerShared, from controller
ExamplesGPS modules, USB serial consoleTemperature sensors, small OLED screensSD cards, fast displays, ADC chips

UART bugs are almost always a wrong baud rate (garbage characters) or TX not crossed to RX. I2C needs pull-up resistors on SDA and SCL (breakout boards usually include them), and the first thing to do with a new I2C device is scan the bus: I2C(0, scl=Pin(22), sda=Pin(21)).scan() lists addresses like [0x48].

Registers and datasheets

Most chips are controlled by reading and writing numbered registers. The datasheet tells you the address, register map and data format. For example, a TMP102 temperature sensor returns two bytes from register 0; the top 12 bits are a two's complement value in units of 0.0625 °C:

raw = (msb << 8 | lsb) >> 4      # 12 bits
if raw & 0x800: raw -= 1 << 12    # negative (two's complement, CS week 1)
celsius = raw * 0.0625

You connect a GPS module over UART and see random garbage characters. What should you check first?

  1. The SPI clock polarity
  2. The pull-up resistors
  3. The baud rate on both sides
  4. The I2C address
UART has no clock, so both ends must agree on the baud rate. A mismatch produces garbage.

You need to connect four temperature sensors using as few pins as possible. Which bus?

  1. I2C, with each sensor at a different address
  2. Four analog pins
  3. SPI
  4. UART
I2C shares two wires among many devices. Many sensors let you set address pins to avoid clashes.

Lesson 2.4 · 35 min

Taming noisy data

Real sensor readings jump around: electrical noise, ADC quirks, a pump switching on, a loose wire. Filtering in firmware is cheap and makes everything downstream (thresholds, graphs, alerts) far more reliable.

  • Oversampling: take several readings quickly and average them. Easy, and it reduces random noise.
  • Moving average over the last N readings: smooth, but it lags behind real changes, and one wild reading still drags it.
  • Median filter over the last N (odd) readings: ignores isolated spikes completely. Excellent for glitches.
  • Exponential moving average (EMA): avg = avg + α × (reading − avg). One variable of memory, so it's perfect for small MCUs. A small α (0.1) is smooth but slow; a large α (0.5) is responsive but noisy.

A common combination is a median of 3–5 to kill spikes, followed by an EMA to smooth.

Also validate: a disconnected sensor often reads 0 or the maximum. Treat readings outside the physically possible range as faults, not data. And use hysteresis for decisions: turn the pump on below 30% but off only above 45%, so noise near one threshold doesn't toggle it rapidly (week 4).

Readings are steady at about 52% but occasionally jump to 0% for one sample. Which filter handles this best?

  1. A moving average
  2. An EMA with α = 0.9
  3. A median filter over 3–5 samples
  4. No filter
The median ignores a single outlier entirely. An average would still be dragged down.

An EMA with α = 0.1 reacts too slowly when you water the plant. What's the trade-off of raising α?

  1. None
  2. It uses more memory
  3. It responds faster but passes through more noise
  4. It becomes a median filter
α trades smoothness for responsiveness.
