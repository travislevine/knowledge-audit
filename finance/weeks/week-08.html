Lesson 8.1 · 50 min

The AI boom through a historical lens

Since late 2022, enthusiasm about artificial intelligence has driven a large part of global stock market gains. Chip maker Nvidia, whose processors train and run AI models, went from a mid-sized company to one of the most valuable in the world, and a handful of US technology giants came to make up roughly a third of the S&P 500. Is it a bubble? Use the tools from weeks 2, 4 and 6 rather than headlines. (Figures change quickly: check current numbers in this week's exercise.)

QuestionDot-com, 2000AI boom, 2020s (as of the mid-2020s)
Are the leaders profitable?Many weren't; leaders like Cisco were, but on P/Es over 100The largest are hugely profitable, with P/Es mostly far below 2000 levels, though some smaller AI stocks trade on hopes alone
Market valuationS&P 500 P/E above 30Elevated, near the high end of history, but driven by companies with real earnings
ConcentrationHighHigher: the top 10 companies are a larger share of the S&P 500 than in 2000
SpendingTelecoms overbuilt fibre networks with debt; most went unused for yearsHundreds of billions a year on data centres and chips, mostly funded from profits so far; the open question is whether AI revenue will justify it
LeverageMargin debt and indebted telecomsLower at the core, though watch increasing debt funding for data centres

A fair conclusion: the AI boom shares several bubble ingredients (a real revolution, extrapolation, concentration, enormous investment) while differing in others (profitability, leverage). History suggests both outcomes are possible: transformative technologies often produce boom-bust cycles in their stocks (railways in the 1840s, radio in the 1920s, the internet in 2000) even when the technology wins. Spending by one company is revenue for another (Nvidia's sales are the hyperscalers' costs), so if spending slows, effects ripple through.

The practical takeaway isn't a prediction. It's to know what you own: an S&P 500 or world index fund today is substantially a bet on a few AI-exposed companies, which is worth understanding before deciding whether that's the risk you want.

Profitability matters, though it doesn't guarantee today's prices are justified.

Lesson 8.2 · 40 min

Reading earnings and the news

Public companies report results every quarter. The share price reacts to results versus expectations, not to whether results are good: a company can grow profits 30% and fall 10% because analysts expected 40%, or because its guidance (forecast for coming quarters) disappointed. What to look at:

  • Revenue (sales) and its growth rate. Is growth accelerating or slowing?
  • Margins: what share of revenue becomes profit, and whether it's rising.
  • Earnings per share versus consensus estimates, and the gap between "adjusted" and official (GAAP) figures. Large, recurring "one-off" adjustments are a warning sign.
  • Free cash flow, and how much is spent on investment (for AI companies, capital expenditure is a key figure).
  • Guidance and management's commentary on demand.

Financial news needs the same scepticism as any other source (Thinking Skills week 2):

  • Daily explanations ("stocks fell on jobs data") are often stories fitted after the fact. Markets move for many reasons at once.
  • Price targets and predictions from strategists have poor track records; the media rewards confident forecasts, not accurate ones.
  • Percentages need context: "Dow drops 800 points" sounds alarming but may be under 2%.
  • Check incentives: brokers profit when you trade, fund managers when you invest with them, and influencers from attention.
  • Survivorship: you hear about the investor who made 50× on one stock, not the thousands who lost.

A company reports earnings up 25% and its shares fall 8%. What's the most likely explanation?

  1. Results or guidance fell short of expectations, which were already built into the price
  2. Earnings don't matter
  3. The market is broken
  4. A computer error
Prices move on surprises relative to expectations.

A headline reads "Dow plunges 900 points". What should you check first?

  1. Nothing: it's a crash
  2. The weather
  3. Whether to sell everything
  4. The percentage change (900 points may be under 2% when the Dow is near 45,000) and the context
Points are meaningless without the level; use percentages.
Lesson 8.3 · 35 min

Investor psychology

Research by firms like Morningstar and Dalbar repeatedly finds that the average investor earns less than the funds they invest in, because they buy after rises and sell after falls. The behaviour gap can be 1–2% a year. The main culprits, many familiar from your Thinking Skills course:

  • Loss aversion: losses feel about twice as painful as gains feel good, so investors panic-sell in downturns and hold losers hoping to "get back to even".
  • Recency and extrapolation: assuming recent trends continue, buying what has just gone up (and was therefore more expensive).
  • FOMO and herding: piling into whatever everyone is talking about. The more a story is everywhere, the more it's likely already in the price.
  • Overconfidence: trading too much. Studies of individual brokerage accounts find the most active traders earn the least.
  • Narrative bias: compelling stories (a visionary founder, a world-changing product) override valuation.
  • Hindsight bias: "everyone knew it was a bubble" — few acted on it at the time, and many who called it did so years too early.

Defences: decide your plan in advance and write it down, automate regular investing, check your portfolio less often, have a rule for rebalancing, and ask "what would make me wrong?" before buying anything. As Warren Buffett put it, be fearful when others are greedy and greedy when others are fearful — simple to say, very hard to do.

Why do average investors tend to earn less than the funds they own?

  1. Funds charge more to individuals
  2. Taxes only
  3. They tend to buy after strong gains and sell after falls, so their timing works against them
  4. Funds hide returns
Behaviour, not fund selection, is often the biggest drag.

A friend made 300% on a meme stock and urges you to buy now. Which biases are most at play?

  1. Loss aversion
  2. FOMO, herding, recency and survivorship (you hear about the winners)
  3. Anchoring only
  4. None
A story everyone knows is usually already priced in.
