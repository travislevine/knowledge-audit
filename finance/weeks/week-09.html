Lesson 9.1 · 35 min

Foundations and principles

This is education, not personal financial advice: everyone's situation differs. But the evidence points to a short list of principles that most independent experts agree on:

  1. Have a foundation first: an emergency fund in cash and no high-interest debt. Paying off a 20% credit card is a guaranteed 20% return.
  2. Match investments to time horizon: money for the next few years in cash or short-term deposits; long-term money can take more risk.
  3. Diversify broadly and cheaply: low-cost index funds or ETFs covering global markets give you the market return and the rare big winners, at minimal cost.
  4. Choose a mix of shares and bonds you can live with: one you'll stick with through a 30–50% fall in the share portion.
  5. Invest regularly (dollar-cost averaging): it automates the habit and avoids agonising over timing.
  6. Minimise costs and taxes, which compound against you.
  7. Rebalance occasionally back to your target mix, which systematically sells what's risen and buys what's fallen.
  8. Stay invested through downturns, and don't let headlines or forecasts change a sound plan.

Individual stock picking can be a fine way to learn, with a small slice of money you can afford to lose: treat it as education. And be wary of anything promising high returns with low risk: in finance, that combination is usually a misunderstanding or a fraud.

You have a credit card debt at 20% interest and $5,000 in savings. What does the evidence generally suggest doing first?

  1. Invest it in shares for growth
  2. Pay off the 20% debt, a guaranteed return no investment reliably beats, while keeping an emergency buffer
  3. Leave it in cash forever
  4. Buy crypto
Guaranteed high-interest savings beat uncertain returns.

Why does rebalancing tend to help long-term investors?

  1. It avoids all losses
  2. It reduces fees
  3. It keeps risk at the level you chose, and systematically sells assets that have risen and buys those that have fallen
  4. It increases returns every year
It's a disciplined counter to chasing performance.
Lesson 9.2 · 40 min

Choosing an ETF

Thousands of ETFs are listed on the ASX and Cboe Australia. For a core long-term holding, compare them on:

  • The index it tracks, which matters most. Australian shares (the ASX 200 or ASX 300), US shares (the S&P 500), global developed markets (MSCI World ex-Australia), emerging markets, or "all-world" funds. Check what's inside: number of holdings, countries and sectors.
  • Management fee (MER). Broad index ETFs in Australia typically charge about 0.04–0.25% a year; thematic, active and smart-beta ETFs often charge 0.5–1%+.
  • Size and liquidity: larger funds from established issuers (Vanguard, iShares/BlackRock, Betashares, SPDR and others) usually have tighter spreads and are less likely to close.
  • Tracking: how closely returns match the index after fees.
  • Currency hedging (week 5): hedged or unhedged versions of global funds.
  • Domicile: some ETFs on the ASX are actually US-domiciled funds, which involves a US tax form (W-8BEN) and different tax handling. Australian-domiciled versions are simpler for most people.
  • Distributions: most ETFs pay income quarterly or half-yearly. A dividend reinvestment plan (DRP) buys more units automatically.

Examples of widely held broad ETFs include ones tracking the ASX 300, the S&P 500, and MSCI World ex-Australia, and "diversified" ETFs that hold a fixed mix of shares and bonds in one fund. Mentioning a type of fund isn't a recommendation: compare current fees and holdings yourself.

Be wary of narrow thematic ETFs (AI, robotics, cybersecurity, lithium): research has found that they tend to launch after their theme has performed well, charge more, and underperform afterwards.

Two ETFs track the same index. One charges 0.07% a year, the other 0.50%. Other things equal, which should a long-term investor prefer, and why?

  1. Whichever has the higher share price
  2. The 0.07% one: tracking the same index, the fee difference goes straight to your return and compounds
  3. The 0.50% one, because it's probably better managed
  4. It doesn't matter
Same index, same return before fees, so the cheaper fund wins.

Why are newly launched thematic ETFs often poor long-term investments?

  1. They're illegal
  2. They tend to launch after a theme has already surged, charge higher fees, and hold concentrated, often expensive stocks
  3. They can't be sold
  4. They only hold bonds
Investors chase what's popular; issuers launch what will sell.

Lesson 9.3 · 40 min

Building a simple portfolio

Most well-built personal portfolios are surprisingly simple: two to four broad ETFs, or a single diversified ETF. A few common shapes (illustrations, not advice):

  • One fund: a diversified ETF holding a fixed mix such as 90% shares and 10% bonds, rebalanced automatically.
  • Two funds: Australian shares plus global shares, for example 30/70.
  • Three or four funds: Australian shares, global developed shares, emerging markets, and a bond ETF, in proportions matching your chosen asset allocation (week 5).

Australia is about 2% of the world's share market, and the ASX is concentrated in banks and miners. Australians often hold more local shares than that (home bias), partly because of franking credits (lesson 9.4), but very high home bias means a concentrated bet on two sectors.

Funding it: investing a set amount regularly (dollar-cost averaging) builds the habit and smooths entry prices. Mathematically, investing a lump sum immediately has beaten spreading it out about two-thirds of the time historically, because markets usually rise; spreading it out mainly reduces regret.

Maintaining it: rebalance back to your target mix once a year, or when a part drifts more than about 5 percentage points. You have a lump sum to invest for 20 years. What does history suggest about investing it all now versus spreading it over 12 months?

  1. Investing it all at once has usually done better, because markets rise more often than they fall; spreading it out mainly reduces regret
  2. Spreading always wins
  3. Waiting for a crash is best
  4. They're always identical
Both are reasonable; know the trade-off.

Your target is 70% shares and 30% bonds. After a strong year you're at 80/20. What does rebalancing do?

  1. Buys more shares
  2. Moves back to 70/30, for example by directing new money to bonds or selling some shares, keeping risk at the level you chose
  3. Nothing
  4. Sells everything
Rebalancing is a built-in 'sell high, buy low'.
Lesson 9.4 · 45 min

Australian practicalities

Brokers and how you hold shares

Australian brokers broadly use one of two models. CHESS-sponsored: your shares and ETFs are registered in your own name under a Holder Identification Number (HIN), so if the broker fails, your holdings are still yours. Custodial: the broker or its custodian holds them on your behalf, which often allows cheaper trading, fractional units or US shares, but adds a dependency on the provider. Compare brokerage per trade, currency conversion fees for overseas shares, and whether the broker is CHESS-sponsored.

Tax basics

  • Capital gains tax (CGT): when you sell for a profit, the gain is added to your taxable income. If you've held the asset for more than 12 months, individuals generally get a 50% CGT discount. Losses can offset gains.
  • Distributions are taxable each year even if reinvested through a DRP, and each reinvestment creates a new parcel with its own cost base: keep records (your annual tax statements, the AMIT member statements, help).
  • Franking credits: Australian companies pay 30% tax (25% for smaller ones), and their dividends can carry credits for tax already paid. You include the credit in your income and get it back as a tax offset; if your tax rate is below the company rate, the excess is refunded. That makes Australian dividends especially valuable to low-tax investors.

Super versus investing outside it

Superannuation is already your largest investment if you work in Australia: employers contribute a percentage of your pay (12% from July 2025) into funds that invest it, mostly in shares. Super is taxed lightly (generally 15% on earnings, with concessional contribution limits) but locked away until preservation age (60 for anyone born after mid-1964). Investing outside super is fully taxed but accessible. Check your super's investment option and fees: they're a big part of your long-term outcome. Moneysmart (moneysmart.gov.au) and the ATO have current rules and calculators.

What's the main protection of a CHESS-sponsored holding?

  1. Lower taxes
  2. Free trading
  3. Guaranteed returns
  4. The shares are registered in your name under your own HIN, so they remain yours if the broker fails
With custodial models you depend more on the provider and its custodian.

You bought ETF units 18 months ago and sell them for a $4,000 gain. As an individual, how much of the gain is generally added to your taxable income?

  1. $1,200
  2. $0
  3. $2,000, after the 50% CGT discount for assets held over 12 months
  4. $4,000
Holding period matters for tax.
