Reading a company

Week 2 showed that a share is worth the cash a business will produce. This week teaches you to look inside the business: read its income statement, balance sheet and cash flow statement, spot the warning signs, and judge whether it has a lasting competitive advantage. You don't need to become an accountant; a handful of numbers tell most of the story.

By the end of this week you can

  • Read an income statement: revenue, gross and operating margins, net income and EPS
  • Read a balance sheet: assets, liabilities, equity, and how much debt is too much
  • Read a cash flow statement, and spot when profits aren't turning into cash
  • Judge a company's competitive advantage (moat), return on capital and management

Lesson 3.1 · 40 min

The income statement

Every listed company publishes three statements each quarter and in its annual report (in the US, the 10-K). The income statement (profit and loss) shows what happened over a period:

Revenue (sales)                       100
− Cost of goods sold                   −40
= Gross profit                          60    gross margin 60%
− Operating expenses (R&D, sales, admin) −35
= Operating income (EBIT)               25    operating margin 25%
− Interest and tax                      −7
= Net income                            18    net margin 18%
÷ Shares outstanding                    → Earnings per share (EPS)

What to look for:

  • Revenue growth, and whether it's from selling more or from acquisitions.
  • Margins and their trend. High gross margins (software 70–90%, luxury goods 60%+) suggest pricing power; supermarkets run on 25% gross and 2–4% net margins. Rising margins as revenue grows show operating leverage: costs grow slower than sales.
  • One-offs and adjustments: companies often highlight "adjusted" earnings or EBITDA (earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation). EBITDA ignores the real cost of replacing equipment; Charlie Munger suggested replacing the word with "bullshit earnings" whenever you see it.
  • Share count: if it rises every year, stock-based pay and new share issues are diluting you, even if net income grows.

A company's revenue grew 20% but its operating margin fell from 25% to 15%. What happened to operating income, roughly?

  1. Rose 20%
  2. Stayed the same
  3. Rose 5%
  4. Fell about 28%: 1.2 × 15 / 25 = 0.72
Margins matter as much as growth. Revenue growth bought at the cost of profitability can shrink profits.

Why should you be cautious when a company emphasises EBITDA rather than net income?

  1. It's always lower
  2. EBITDA is illegal
  3. It's only used by banks
  4. It leaves out interest, tax and the cost of wearing out equipment, which are real costs, so it can make a business look more profitable than it is
EBITDA is useful for comparing operations, but capital-heavy companies can look healthy on EBITDA while burning cash.

Lesson 3.2 · 40 min

The balance sheet

The balance sheet is a snapshot at one moment: what the company owns (assets), what it owes (liabilities), and the difference, which belongs to shareholders (equity). It always balances: assets = liabilities + equity.

  • Current assets: cash, receivables (money customers owe), inventory. Non-current: property, equipment, and intangibles like goodwill (the premium paid for acquisitions, which can be written down if a deal goes badly).
  • Liabilities: payables, short-term and long-term debt, leases.

The questions that matter most:

  • How much debt? Net debt = debt − cash. A common rule of thumb compares it to annual operating profit: net debt / EBITDA under about 2–3 is usually comfortable for a steady business; above 4–5 is risky, especially for cyclical companies. Check when the debt is due: a big refinancing in a crisis can sink an otherwise good business.
  • Can it pay its bills? The current ratio (current assets ÷ current liabilities) shows short-term liquidity.
  • Interest cover: operating income ÷ interest. Below about 3, a downturn could make interest hard to pay.
  • Warning signs: receivables or inventory growing much faster than sales (customers not paying, or products not selling), or a large goodwill balance from expensive acquisitions.

Debt isn't automatically bad: it can boost returns to shareholders. But it removes the margin for error. Most corporate collapses in a recession are companies with too much debt due at the wrong time.

A company has $10bn of debt, $2bn of cash and $2bn of annual EBITDA. What's its net debt to EBITDA, and how does that look?

  1. 4×: at the risky end for most businesses
  2. 6×: normal for any company
  3. It can't be calculated
  4. 1×: very safe
(10 − 2) / 2 = 4×. Above about 3× deserves scrutiny, especially in cyclical industries.

Receivables have grown 50% in a year while revenue grew 10%. What might that signal?

  1. Strong sales
  2. Lower costs
  3. A share buyback
  4. Customers are taking longer to pay, or sales are being booked aggressively before cash arrives
Receivables racing ahead of revenue is a classic warning sign worth investigating.
Lesson 3.3 · 35 min

The cash flow statement

Profit is an opinion; cash is a fact. Accounting rules let companies recognise revenue before cash arrives and spread costs over years, so net income can look healthy while the business burns cash. The cash flow statement shows the actual money moving, in three parts:

  • Operating cash flow: cash generated by the business itself.
  • Investing cash flow: spending on equipment and buildings (capital expenditure, capex), and acquisitions.
  • Financing cash flow: borrowing and repaying debt, issuing or buying back shares, and paying dividends.

Free cash flow (FCF) = operating cash flow − capex. It's the cash available for shareholders: for dividends, buybacks, paying down debt or acquisitions. Investors often compare it to market cap as the free cash flow yield.

Warning signs:

  • Net income consistently well above operating cash flow. Enron and Wirecard both reported profits that never turned into cash.
  • A company that pays dividends funded by borrowing (financing inflows) rather than free cash flow.
  • Heavy and rising capex just to stand still, common in airlines, telecoms and car makers.
  • Stock-based compensation added back to operating cash flow: it's a real cost paid in shares rather than cash.

For AI-era companies, capex is the key line: the largest tech companies are spending enormous sums on data centres, which lowers free cash flow today in the hope of higher profits later.

A company reports $500m of net income but only $50m of operating cash flow, year after year. What should you think?

  1. Very profitable
  2. It should pay a bigger dividend
  3. A red flag: profits aren't turning into cash, which can mean aggressive accounting or worse
  4. Normal for all companies
Persistent gaps between profit and cash are how many accounting frauds were first spotted.

What is free cash flow?

  1. Dividends paid
  2. Cash in the bank
  3. Revenue minus costs
  4. Operating cash flow minus capital expenditure: the cash left for shareholders
It's the cash the business could return to owners without shrinking.
Lesson 3.4 · 40 min

Moats and quality

Numbers describe the past; the question is whether a business can keep earning high returns. Warren Buffett calls a durable competitive advantage a moat: something that stops competitors from taking its profits. The main kinds:

  • Network effects: the product gets better as more people use it (payment networks, marketplaces, social platforms).
  • Switching costs: changing is painful (enterprise software, banking, operating systems).
  • Intangible assets: brands people pay more for, patents, licences.
  • Cost advantages: scale, unique processes or locations that let it sell cheaper and still profit.

The best evidence of a moat is a high return on invested capital (ROIC) sustained for many years: operating profit after tax divided by the capital tied up in the business. A company earning 25% on its capital for a decade, when competitors earn 8%, probably has one. Return on equity (ROE) is similar but can be flattered by debt.

Also judge management and capital allocation: do they reinvest at high returns, buy back shares when cheap (not just when popular), avoid expensive empire-building acquisitions, and talk candidly about mistakes? Is their pay tied to long-term per-share results?

A quick analysis framework: what does it sell and to whom; how does it make money; is it growing; is it profitable and cash-generative; how much debt; what's the moat; what could go wrong; and what is the price assuming? A wonderful business can still be a bad investment at the wrong price (week 6, Cisco).

Which is the strongest evidence that a company has a durable moat?

  1. Returns on invested capital well above competitors' for many years
  2. Rapid revenue growth last year
  3. A famous CEO
  4. A high share price
Competition normally drives returns down. Sustained high returns suggest something is stopping it.

A payments network becomes more useful to shoppers as more shops accept it, and to shops as more shoppers use it. What kind of moat is this?

  1. Patent
  2. Network effect
  3. Switching cost
  4. Cost advantage
Two-sided networks are among the strongest moats.
Scenario exercise · 60–90 minutes

Scenario exercise: analyse a real company

Pick a listed company you know (for example a supermarket, a bank, a tech company or a miner). Find its latest annual report or a site like Morningstar or Yahoo Finance, and in finance/exercises/week-03.md:

  1. Income statement. Revenue and its growth over 3–5 years, gross and operating margins and their trend, EPS, and whether the share count is rising.
  2. Balance sheet. Net debt, net debt to EBITDA, and anything unusual (big goodwill, receivables growing fast).
  3. Cash flow. Operating cash flow versus net income, capex, free cash flow, and how the company used its cash.
  4. Moat and management. What, if anything, protects its profits? Is ROIC high and stable? How has management allocated capital?
  5. Verdict. At today's price (P/E or free cash flow yield), what growth is the market assuming? Is that realistic? What could go wrong?

There are no right answers to look up: the point is to apply the week to real markets and real history. Write in your own words. This is education, not financial advice.
