Beyond shares: other asset classes

Shares are only one part of the investing world. This week covers the other major asset classes: what each one is, where its returns come from, how it behaves in booms, crashes and inflation, and how mixing them changes the ride. The key idea is that each asset earns its return for a different reason, which is what makes combining them useful.

By the end of this week you can

  • Explain the main types of bonds, their risks, and their role in a portfolio
  • Explain how property and REITs, commodities and gold earn (or don't earn) returns
  • Describe currency risk and the speculative nature of crypto assets
  • Explain asset allocation, and how mixing assets changes return and risk

Lesson 5.1 · 40 min

Bonds and cash in practice

Week 4 covered how bond prices and yields move. In practice, the bond world splits into a few kinds:

  • Government bonds (US Treasuries, Australian Commonwealth bonds, German Bunds): the safest borrowers in their own currency. They tend to rise in value when shares fall in a recession, because central banks cut rates.
  • Inflation-linked bonds (TIPS in the US): principal rises with inflation, protecting purchasing power.
  • Investment-grade corporate bonds: companies rated BBB or above. A little more yield for a little default risk.
  • High-yield ("junk") bonds: weaker companies, higher yields, and they behave much like shares in a crisis.

Bond risks: interest rate risk (duration, week 4), credit risk (default), and inflation risk (fixed payments lose real value). A bond's yield to maturity at purchase is a good estimate of the return you'll earn if you hold it to the end and it doesn't default. That's why bonds became far more attractive after 2022: yields rose from near zero to 4–5%.

Cash (savings accounts, term deposits, money market funds) has no price risk, but after inflation and tax its real return has often been close to zero. It's the right place for an emergency fund and money you'll need soon, not for long-term growth.

The role of high-quality bonds in a portfolio is mostly stability: they cushion falls in shares and give you something to sell, or rebalance from, when shares are cheap. The exception is an inflation shock, as 2022 showed, when bonds and shares fell together.

In a typical recession with falling inflation, how do high-quality government bonds usually behave?

  1. They default
  2. They usually rise, as central banks cut rates, cushioning a portfolio
  3. They're unaffected
  4. They fall with shares
That's why they're held as a stabiliser, with the 2022 inflation shock as the notable exception.

Why do high-yield (junk) bonds offer less protection in a crash than government bonds?

  1. They're illiquid
  2. They're inflation-linked
  3. They're shorter
  4. Their default risk rises in a downturn, so they tend to fall alongside shares
Credit risk and equity risk are close cousins.
Lesson 5.2 · 40 min

Property, commodities and gold

Property earns returns from rent plus changes in value. Direct property ownership is large, concentrated, illiquid and usually leveraged (a mortgage), which is why it can feel safe (no daily price) while being quite risky. Real estate investment trusts (REITs) are listed companies that own property portfolios (shopping centres, offices, warehouses, data centres) and pay out most of their income. They give property exposure in small, liquid amounts, but trade like shares and fall with the market in a crisis.

Commodities (oil, gas, metals, agricultural products) are raw materials. They produce no income, so long-run returns come from price changes, which follow supply and demand cycles and can be extreme. Oil fell below zero for a day in April 2020 when storage ran out, then more than doubled within two years. Commodity prices often rise with inflation, which is when shares and bonds struggle. Many investors get exposure through mining and energy companies instead.

Gold also produces no income. It's held as a store of value and crisis hedge: it tends to do well when real interest rates are low or falling, when confidence in currencies or governments is shaken, and when central banks are buying. Over very long periods it has roughly kept pace with inflation, with long stretches of poor returns (it fell about 70% from its 1980 peak to 2001, before even counting inflation). Warren Buffett's critique: all the gold ever mined would fit in a cube about 22 metres on each side, and it will never produce anything.

The distinction to remember: productive assets (businesses, property, bonds) generate cash; non-productive assets (gold, most commodities, art, crypto) only return what the next buyer will pay.

What's the main difference between owning a REIT and owning an investment property directly?

  1. REITs don't own property
  2. A REIT is diversified, liquid and available in small amounts, but its price moves daily with the share market
  3. REITs pay no income
  4. Direct property is always less risky
Direct property only feels less volatile because it isn't priced every day.

Why do commodities and gold behave differently from shares over the long run?

  1. They're more regulated
  2. They always rise with inflation
  3. They're traded only by governments
  4. They produce no cash flows, so their returns depend entirely on price changes driven by supply, demand and sentiment
Productive versus non-productive assets.
Lesson 5.3 · 35 min

Currencies and crypto

When you own foreign assets, you also own the foreign currency. If you buy US shares with Australian dollars and the US dollar rises 10% against the Australian dollar, you gain 10% even if the shares don't move; if it falls, you lose. Over long periods currency moves roughly wash out, but they add volatility. Currency-hedged funds remove most of this effect at a small cost. For shares, many investors leave currency unhedged, partly because the Australian dollar tends to fall in global crises, which cushions losses on foreign assets; for foreign bonds, hedging is common, since currency swings would swamp their modest returns.

Currencies move with interest rate differences, trade, commodity prices and risk appetite. The US dollar is the world's reserve currency: it tends to strengthen in global panics as investors seek safety.

Crypto assets like Bitcoin are digital tokens recorded on a blockchain. Bitcoin has a fixed maximum supply of 21 million coins, which supporters compare to digital gold. Facts worth knowing:

  • It produces no cash flow, so there's no fundamental valuation anchor: price depends on adoption and belief.
  • It's extremely volatile, with multiple falls of 70–85% (2014, 2018, 2022).
  • It has repeatedly fallen alongside speculative tech stocks, so it hasn't reliably behaved as a hedge.
  • The industry has a long record of collapses and frauds (Mt. Gox, Terra/Luna, Celsius, FTX), mostly involving centralised platforms holding customers' coins. "Not your keys, not your coins."
  • Spot Bitcoin ETFs launched in the US in January 2024, making it easier to hold in a brokerage account.

Whatever your view, treat crypto as a speculative asset: size any position so that losing 80% of it wouldn't change your life.

You hold unhedged US shares. They're flat over a year, but the Australian dollar falls 10% against the US dollar. What's your return in Australian dollars, roughly?

  1. +5%
  2. 0%
  3. About +11%
  4. −10%
Each US dollar is now worth more Australian dollars: 1 / 0.9 ≈ 1.11.

Which statement about Bitcoin is best supported by its history?

  1. It can't be bought through regulated products
  2. It's a reliable crisis hedge
  3. It has no cash flows, has had several 70%+ falls, and has often fallen alongside speculative tech stocks
  4. Its value is guaranteed by its fixed supply
Evidence, not narratives.
Lesson 5.4 · 40 min

Asset allocation

Asset allocation, how you divide money between shares, bonds, cash and other assets, determines most of a diversified portfolio's long-run risk and return. Studies of pension funds famously found it explained the large majority of the variation in their returns over time, far more than which individual securities they picked.

The classic trade-off: more shares mean higher expected long-run return and deeper falls. Try it with approximate US history since 1926:

Common approaches:

  • A fixed mix such as 80/20 or 60/40 (shares/bonds), rebalanced periodically.
  • Age-based or "glide path" mixes that hold more shares when young and shift towards bonds as a goal approaches. Target-date funds and many superannuation "lifecycle" options do this automatically.
  • Risk parity (Ray Dalio's All Weather approach): balance risk rather than dollars across assets that do well in different environments (growth, recession, inflation, deflation), often using leverage on bonds.

The right mix depends less on forecasts and more on you: your time horizon, how stable your income is, and above all how much of a fall you can live through without selling. A 90% share portfolio that you abandon in a crash does worse than a 60% one you hold.

What mainly determines a diversified portfolio's long-run risk and return?

  1. Its asset allocation: the mix of shares, bonds, cash and other assets
  2. How often it's checked
  3. The broker used
  4. Picking the best individual stocks
Get the mix right first; details second.

Two investors: A holds 100% shares but sells in every crash; B holds 60% shares and never sells. Who is likely to end up better off?

  1. Often B: the higher expected return of shares only arrives for those who stay invested through falls
  2. A, because shares return more
  3. They'll be identical
  4. Neither will make money
The best allocation is one you can stick with.
