What moves markets

Individual companies matter, but the biggest market swings come from a few economy-wide forces. This week explains why markets hang on every word from the US Federal Reserve, why rising interest rates can push stock prices down even when companies are doing well, how bonds work, and how debt, the business cycle and investor mood interact.

By the end of this week you can

  • Explain how central banks set interest rates and why rates affect stock valuations
  • Explain inflation, its causes, and why markets fear it
  • Explain how bonds work, why bond prices fall when yields rise, and what the yield curve signals
  • Describe the business and credit cycle, and the role of sentiment and liquidity

Lesson 4.1 · 45 min

Central banks and interest rates

Central banks (the US Federal Reserve, the European Central Bank, the Bank of England, the Bank of Japan) set the short-term interest rate in their economies. They raise rates to cool an overheating economy and fight inflation, and cut them to support growth in a slowdown. In a crisis, they also buy bonds (quantitative easing, QE) to push longer-term rates down and pump money into the financial system. Reversing it is quantitative tightening.

Interest rates are sometimes called gravity for asset prices. Three reasons:

  • Discounting: a share is worth its future cash discounted to today. Higher rates mean future cash is worth less now. Companies whose profits are mostly far in the future (fast-growing tech) are hit hardest, which is why the Nasdaq fell about a third in 2022.
  • Competition: when cash and bonds pay 5%, investors need a better reason to own shares than when they pay 0%.
  • The economy: higher borrowing costs slow spending, investment and hiring, which eventually reduces profits.

That's why markets react not only to rate decisions but to expectations of them. A rate rise that's fully expected often moves nothing; a hint that rates will stay high for longer can move markets more than the decision itself. Markets are forward-looking: prices reflect what investors expect, so what moves them is surprise.

Why did fast-growing tech stocks fall more than the average stock when interest rates rose sharply in 2022?

  1. Regulators targeted them
  2. Tech companies pay more tax
  3. Their value depends more on profits far in the future, which higher discount rates shrink the most
  4. Tech companies borrow more
The further away the cash flows, the more sensitive the present value is to the discount rate.

The central bank raises rates by 0.25%, exactly as everyone expected, and the market barely moves. Why?

  1. Rates don't matter
  2. The rise was already reflected in prices; markets move on surprises and changes in expectations
  3. The market was closed
  4. 0.25% is too small to matter
Watch what's expected, not just what happens.
Lesson 4.2 · 35 min

Inflation

Inflation is a general rise in prices, usually measured by the consumer price index (CPI). Central banks typically target about 2% a year. Inflation rises when demand outruns supply: after heavy government spending and low rates, after supply shocks (oil in the 1970s, pandemic supply chains and the 2022 energy shock), or when wages and prices chase each other.

Why markets fear it:

  • It erodes the real value of cash and fixed bond payments.
  • It forces central banks to raise rates, which hurts valuations and can cause recessions. Fed chair Paul Volcker raised rates to nearly 20% in 1981 to break 1970s inflation, triggering a deep recession.
  • It squeezes companies that can't pass on rising costs. Businesses with pricing power (strong brands, essential products) cope best.

Shares are a reasonable long-run inflation hedge, because companies own real assets and can raise prices, but in the short run high and rising inflation has usually been bad for both shares and bonds. In 2022, with US inflation around 9%, both fell together, an unusually painful year for balanced portfolios.

Deflation, falling prices, is feared too: it makes debts harder to repay and encourages people to delay spending. It was a major feature of the Great Depression and of Japan's lost decades.

Inflation jumps unexpectedly. What's the most likely sequence?

  1. Markets expect the central bank to raise rates; bond prices and many stock prices fall
  2. Nothing changes
  3. Central bank cuts rates; stocks rise
  4. Only commodity prices change
Inflation surprises usually mean higher expected rates.

Which company is best placed to cope with high inflation?

  1. A fast-growing company with no profits
  2. One with strong pricing power, such as an essential product or dominant brand, and low debt
  3. A company that has fixed-price contracts for 10 years
  4. One with heavy debt at floating rates and no pricing power
Pricing power lets a company pass on rising costs.
Lesson 4.3 · 40 min

Bonds and the yield curve

A bond is a loan. You lend a government or company, say, $1,000; it pays fixed interest (the coupon) and returns the $1,000 at maturity. Government bonds from stable countries are treated as the safest assets; corporate bonds pay more because the borrower could default.

The key idea: bond prices move opposite to interest rates. If you own a bond paying 3% and new bonds pay 5%, nobody will pay full price for yours, so its price falls until its yield (the return a new buyer gets) matches 5%. Longer bonds are more sensitive. Try it:

The 10-year US Treasury yield is one of the most important numbers in finance: it's the "risk-free" rate used to value almost everything else, including shares and mortgages.

The yield curve plots yields across maturities. Normally longer bonds yield more (investors want extra for tying money up). When short-term yields exceed long-term ones, the curve is inverted: markets expect rates to fall, usually because they expect a slowdown. An inverted US curve has preceded every US recession since the late 1960s, though the timing varies widely and there has been at least one false alarm.

The bond market is larger than the stock market, and its moves often lead. Watch credit spreads too: the extra yield on corporate bonds over government bonds widens when investors fear defaults, an early sign of stress.

You hold a 10-year bond paying 3%. Market interest rates for similar bonds rise to 5%. What happens to your bond's price?

  1. It falls, so a new buyer's yield matches 5%
  2. It stays at face value
  3. It rises
  4. It defaults
Prices and yields move in opposite directions.

What does an inverted yield curve usually signal?

  1. Stock prices will rise immediately
  2. High inflation ahead
  3. An economic boom
  4. Markets expect rates to fall, typically because they expect a slowdown or recession
It has a strong record as a recession signal, with unreliable timing.
Lesson 4.4 · 40 min

Cycles, debt and sentiment

Economies move in business cycles: expansion, peak, contraction (recession), recovery. Company profits rise and fall with them, and the stock market usually moves before the economy. It often falls months before a recession starts and starts rising while the news is still bad, because it prices expected future profits.

Underneath sits the credit cycle, which Ray Dalio's video explains well. Borrowing lets spending grow faster than income for a while: easy credit lifts asset prices, rising prices make lenders comfortable lending more, and so on. Eventually debts become too large relative to incomes, lending tightens, and the process reverses, sometimes violently (a deleveraging). Most severe crashes are credit crashes.

Sentiment amplifies everything. In good times investors extrapolate, lower their guard and borrow; in bad times they panic and sell regardless of value. Economist Hyman Minsky argued that stability itself breeds instability: long calm periods encourage the risk-taking that causes the next crisis. Indicators like the VIX ("the fear index", which measures expected volatility), margin debt and fund flows give a rough read on mood.

Liquidity (how much money is available and how easily assets can be sold) matters too: when everyone wants to sell at once, prices can gap down far further than fundamentals justify, as in March 2020.

Other forces: currencies (a strong US dollar tightens conditions globally), commodities (oil shocks), geopolitics, and government fiscal policy (spending and taxes).

The economy is in recession and unemployment is still rising, yet the stock market has risen 25% from its low. How is that possible?

  1. The market is irrational
  2. Markets look ahead: prices reflect expected future profits, so they often recover before the economic news does
  3. Only tech stocks rose
  4. Stock prices are random
Waiting for good economic news usually means missing much of the recovery.

What is Minsky's insight about financial stability?

  1. Central banks prevent all crises
  2. Stability is permanent
  3. Markets are always efficient
  4. Long periods of stability encourage more borrowing and risk-taking, which sets up the next crisis
Calm periods breed complacency.
