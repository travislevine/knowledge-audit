Lesson 10.1 · 40 min

Graham and Buffett: value investing

Benjamin Graham, Buffett's teacher, wrote The Intelligent Investor (1949). His key ideas: a share is part of a business, not a ticker symbol; the market is like a moody business partner, Mr. Market, who offers wildly different prices each day, and you can take advantage of his mood rather than be led by it; and always buy with a margin of safety, a price well below your estimate of value, so errors don't ruin you.

Warren Buffett refined this: rather than buying mediocre companies very cheaply, buy wonderful businesses at fair prices: companies with durable moats (week 3), honest, capable management, and high returns on capital, then hold them for decades. Berkshire Hathaway compounded at roughly 20% a year from 1965 to 2024, about double the S&P 500, turning into one of the world's largest companies. Other principles: stay within your circle of competence, be "fearful when others are greedy", and avoid leverage. In 2025 he announced that Greg Abel would succeed him as CEO.

The twist: Buffett has repeatedly said that most people, professionals included, should simply own a low-cost S&P 500 index fund, and his will instructs that most of the money left for his wife go into one. His record is an argument for how hard stock picking is, not how easy.

His annual shareholder letters, free on Berkshire's website, are among the best business writing available.

What is Graham's "margin of safety"?

  1. Holding cash
  2. A stop-loss order
  3. Diversifying across 30 stocks
  4. Buying well below your estimate of a business's value, so that mistakes or bad luck don't cause big losses
It's protection against your own errors.

What does Buffett recommend for most investors?

  1. Day trading
  2. Gold
  3. Copying Berkshire's trades
  4. A low-cost S&P 500 index fund
The world's most famous stock picker recommends not picking stocks.

Lesson 10.2 · 45 min

Other legends: Bogle, Lynch, Soros, Dalio, Simons

  • John Bogle founded Vanguard and launched the first index fund for ordinary investors in 1976, mocked at the time as "Bogle's folly". His message: "Don't look for the needle in the haystack. Just buy the haystack." Costs matter, because you get what you don't pay for. Index funds now hold trillions of dollars, and Bogle probably saved investors more money than anyone in history.
  • Peter Lynch ran Fidelity's Magellan fund from 1977 to 1990 at about 29% a year. He's known for "invest in what you know": spotting good companies in everyday life. He insisted that was only the starting point for doing the homework (week 3), not a reason to buy.
  • George Soros is a macro trader famous for "breaking the Bank of England" in 1992, making about $1 billion by betting that the UK couldn't keep the pound in Europe's exchange-rate mechanism. His theory of reflexivity: prices don't just reflect fundamentals, they change them (rising share prices let companies raise money and grow), which feeds booms and busts.
  • Ray Dalio built Bridgewater into the world's largest hedge fund using debt-cycle analysis (week 4) and the All Weather portfolio (week 5).
  • Jim Simons, a mathematician, founded Renaissance Technologies, whose Medallion fund (week 7) found patterns with computers and statistics: the clearest proof that markets aren't perfectly efficient, and that the remaining edges go to those with extraordinary resources.

Common threads: each had a clear, consistent method, managed risk, and thought independently. Their records are also extreme outliers selected from thousands of people who tried. For ordinary investors, the most transferable lessons are Bogle's (low costs, broad diversification) and the shared emphasis on discipline and avoiding ruin.

What was Bogle's central insight?

  1. Buy gold
  2. Pick the best managers
  3. Time the market
  4. Costs are a sure drag on returns, so owning the whole market cheaply beats most attempts to outperform it
"Buy the haystack."

What does Soros mean by reflexivity?

  1. Markets are random
  2. Traders copy each other
  3. Prices always reflect fundamentals
  4. Prices can change the fundamentals they're supposed to reflect, creating self-reinforcing booms and busts
It helps explain bubbles (week 6).
Lesson 10.3 · 40 min

Frauds and scams

Frauds succeed by exploiting trust, greed and fear of missing out. Famous examples:

  • Bernie Madoff ran the largest Ponzi scheme known, with paper losses of about $65 billion, revealed in 2008. Investors received steady returns of about 1% a month year after year, which were paid from new investors' money. The returns were too smooth to be real, and the strategy couldn't be explained. Analyst Harry Markopolos warned the SEC for years.
  • Enron (2001) hid debt in off-balance-sheet entities and reported profits that never became cash (week 3). Its auditor, Arthur Andersen, collapsed with it.
  • Wirecard (2020), a German payments company, admitted that €1.9 billion of cash on its balance sheet probably didn't exist, after years of reports from journalists and short sellers.
  • FTX (2022), a major crypto exchange, secretly used customers' deposits for its affiliated trading firm's bets. Founder Sam Bankman-Fried was convicted of fraud in 2023 and sentenced to 25 years.

Retail scams follow the same patterns, and in Australia investment scams cost people hundreds of millions of dollars a year: fake trading platforms, crypto "opportunities" promoted on social media (often with fake celebrity endorsements), romance scams that turn into investing, and impersonations of real firms.

Red flags:

  • High returns with little or no risk, or unusually consistent returns.
  • Pressure to act fast, secrecy, or "exclusive" access.
  • Strategies you can't understand or verify, and returns you can't easily withdraw.
  • Unlicensed sellers: in Australia, check ASIC's professional register and Moneysmart's list of companies to avoid.
  • Contact you didn't initiate, especially through social media or messaging apps.

What was the clearest warning sign in Madoff's results?

  1. He charged high fees
  2. He was famous
  3. They were too low
  4. Returns were unrealistically smooth and steady for years, from a strategy no one could replicate
Real investments have bad months.

Someone on social media offers a crypto trading service with "guaranteed 3% a week" and urges you to deposit today. What should you do?

  1. Treat it as a scam: guaranteed high returns plus urgency are classic red flags; check ASIC's register and Moneysmart before engaging
  2. Ask for a bigger guarantee
  3. Deposit a small amount to test it
  4. Invest with leverage
Small 'test' deposits that pay out are how many scams build trust.
Lesson 10.4 · 35 min

Putting it all together

Ten weeks in one page:

  1. A share is a slice of a business; its value comes from future cash, and prices swing around value with sentiment (weeks 1–3).
  2. The whole market is moved by interest rates, inflation, credit and mood, and it looks ahead, reacting to surprises (week 4).
  3. Different assets earn returns for different reasons; mixing them changes the ride (week 5).
  4. Booms built on a real story, easy money and leverage end in busts; leverage and policy decide how bad (week 6).
  5. Most active traders and funds trail the market after costs; the reliable winners are those selling services and those with extraordinary resources (week 7).
  6. Today's market is concentrated in a few AI-exposed giants: understand what you own (week 8).
  7. For individuals, the evidence favours foundations first, broad low-cost diversification, a mix you can stick with, regular investing, and staying the course (week 9).
  8. Great investors are rare outliers with discipline and risk control; frauds exploit greed and trust (week 10).

The skill that ties it together is the one from your Thinking Skills course: separate evidence from stories, think in probabilities, and know your own biases. Markets reward patience and humility far more reliably than cleverness.
