Bubbles and crashes

Every generation believes its boom is different. This week walks through the biggest crashes of the last century: what happened, why, how far markets fell and how long they took to recover. Then it pulls out the recurring patterns, which are the most useful thing you can know about market history.

By the end of this week you can

  • Explain the causes and consequences of the 1929 crash and the Great Depression
  • Explain the 1987 crash, the dot-com bust and the 2008 financial crisis, and what each taught
  • Contrast the 2020 COVID crash and the 2022 rate-driven bear market with earlier crashes
  • Identify the recurring ingredients of bubbles and crashes, and how recoveries differ

Lesson 6.1 · 40 min

1929 and the Great Depression

The 1920s were a boom: new technologies (cars, radio, electricity), rising profits and a belief in a "new era" of permanent prosperity. Ordinary people bought shares on margin, often borrowing 90% of the price. The Dow Jones rose about sixfold from 1921 to its peak in September 1929.

In October 1929 prices broke. On Black Thursday (24 October) and Black Monday and Tuesday (28–29 October) the market collapsed. Margin calls forced leveraged investors to sell, which pushed prices lower and triggered more margin calls. By July 1932 the Dow had fallen about 89% from its peak.

The crash alone didn't cause the Great Depression; policy mistakes turned a bust into a catastrophe:

  • About 9,000 US banks failed in the early 1930s, wiping out depositors (there was no deposit insurance), so money and credit shrank by about a third.
  • The Federal Reserve let it happen, and the gold standard limited what it could do.
  • Tariffs (the Smoot-Hawley Act of 1930) and retaliation shrank world trade.
  • Prices fell (deflation), making debts heavier.

US unemployment reached about 25%. The Dow didn't regain its 1929 peak until 1954, 25 years later, although with dividends and falling prices included, real investors who held on recovered sooner (in the mid-1930s to early 1940s, depending on the measure).

Legacies: deposit insurance, securities regulation and the SEC, limits on margin lending, and the lesson that central banks must act as lender of last resort in a crisis, which shaped the response in 2008.

What turned the 1929 crash into a decade-long depression?

  1. High interest rates in 1928 only
  2. The crash alone
  3. Policy failures: thousands of bank failures with no deposit insurance, a passive central bank, the gold standard, tariffs and deflation
  4. War
Ben Bernanke (later Fed chair during 2008) built his career studying this.

Why did margin lending make the 1929 crash so severe?

  1. It only affected banks
  2. It was illegal
  3. Falling prices triggered margin calls, forcing sales that pushed prices lower and triggered more margin calls
  4. It kept prices high
Leverage turns a fall into a spiral.
Lesson 6.2 · 45 min

1987 and the dot-com bust

Black Monday, 1987

On 19 October 1987 the Dow fell 22.6% in a single day, still the largest one-day percentage fall in its history. Markets had risen strongly that year; rising interest rates and a weak dollar made investors nervous. The crash was amplified by portfolio insurance, a computerised strategy that automatically sold futures as prices fell, creating a feedback loop. The Fed promptly promised liquidity, the economy didn't enter recession, and the market regained its pre-crash level within about two years. Lessons: market structure and automated selling can cause crashes without an economic cause, and exchanges later added circuit breakers that pause trading after large falls.

The dot-com bubble, 1995–2002

The internet was a real revolution, and that's what made the bubble so convincing. Investors poured money into any company with ".com" in its name, many with no profits and little revenue. Companies were valued on "eyeballs" and page views. The Nasdaq rose about fivefold from 1995 to its peak in March 2000, when the S&P 500's P/E exceeded 30. Cisco briefly became the world's most valuable company on a P/E above 100.

Then rising interest rates, companies running out of cash, and accounting scandals (Enron in 2001, WorldCom in 2002) burst it. The Nasdaq fell about 78% by October 2002 and didn't regain its peak until 2015. The S&P 500 fell about 49%.

The crucial lesson: a technology can change the world and still be a terrible investment at the wrong price. Amazon survived but fell about 94%; Pets.com and hundreds of others vanished. Cisco remained a highly profitable company, yet its share price two decades later was still well below its 2000 peak, because the price had assumed impossible growth.

What made the 1987 crash unusual compared with 1929 or 2008?

  1. It was caused by bank failures
  2. It was a sudden market-structure event amplified by automated selling, with no recession following, and recovered in about two years
  3. It only hit tech stocks
  4. It lasted years
Not every crash reflects a broken economy.

Cisco stayed profitable after 2000, yet its shares took decades (if ever) to regain their peak. What's the lesson?

  1. Profits don't matter
  2. A great company can be a poor investment if the price already assumes unrealistic growth
  3. Cisco was a fraud
  4. Tech companies never recover
Price paid determines return.
Lesson 6.3 · 45 min

The 2008 global financial crisis

The 2008 crisis was a classic credit bubble, built in housing and hidden in the banking system.

  1. Cheap credit and a housing boom. After 2001, low interest rates and a belief that house prices never fall nationally fuelled borrowing. Lenders made subprime mortgages to borrowers who couldn't afford them, often with low "teaser" rates.
  2. Securitisation. Banks bundled mortgages into securities (MBS, CDOs) and sold them worldwide. Credit rating agencies rated many of them AAA. Lenders who wouldn't hold the loans stopped caring whether they'd be repaid.
  3. Leverage. Investment banks borrowed 30 or more times their capital. At 30:1, a 3.3% fall in asset values wipes out all equity.
  4. The bust. As rates rose and teaser periods ended, defaults climbed and house prices fell about 27%. Nobody knew who held the losses, so banks stopped lending to each other.
  5. Panic. Bear Stearns was rescued in March 2008. In September, Lehman Brothers was allowed to fail, and AIG, Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac were bailed out. Credit markets froze worldwide.

The S&P 500 fell about 57% from October 2007 to March 2009. Governments and central banks responded massively: bank bailouts (TARP), interest rates near zero, quantitative easing and stimulus. The S&P regained its peak in 2013; total-return investors recovered somewhat sooner.

Lessons: leverage plus assets everyone thinks are safe is the most dangerous combination; complexity hides risk; incentives matter (originate-to-distribute lending, bonuses for short-term gains); and decisive central bank action can stop a depression, though it leaves debates about moral hazard and inequality. Regulation tightened, with higher bank capital requirements and stress tests.

Why were investment banks so fragile going into 2008?

  1. They held too much cash
  2. They had no customers
  3. They were leveraged 30 or more to 1, so a small percentage fall in their assets could wipe out their equity
  4. Interest rates were too high
Leverage turned mortgage losses into a banking crisis.

How did securitisation contribute to the crisis?

  1. It reduced risk
  2. Lenders sold loans on rather than holding them, weakening their incentive to lend carefully, and spread hard-to-value risk throughout the global system
  3. It only affected the US
  4. It made mortgages illegal
When you don't bear the risk of a decision, you take more of it.

Lesson 6.4 · 40 min

2020, 2022 and the patterns

COVID, 2020

From 19 February to 23 March 2020 the S&P 500 fell about 34% in 33 days, the fastest bear market in history, as economies shut down. Then central banks cut rates to zero and bought assets on an enormous scale, governments spent trillions, and the market regained its peak by August 2020, within about six months. Investors who sold in March locked in the loss and often missed the rebound.

The 2022 rate shock

Stimulus, supply disruptions and an energy shock pushed US inflation to about 9%. The Fed raised rates from near zero to over 5% in about 16 months, its fastest tightening in four decades. The S&P 500 fell about 25%, the Nasdaq about 35%, and bonds had one of their worst years on record. Speculative assets (unprofitable tech, meme stocks, crypto) fell 60–90%. The S&P regained its peak in early 2024, led by AI enthusiasm.

What crashes have in common

Bubbles usually combine: (1) a real innovation or story, (2) easy money and leverage, (3) a "this time is different" narrative and extrapolation of recent gains, (4) new, inexperienced investors and new financial products, and (5) valuations far above history. Crashes are triggered by tightening (rising rates, a credit event) and accelerated by forced selling. How deep and long they are depends on leverage and policy response: the debt-driven crashes (1929, 2008) were the worst.

And the most important practical pattern: the best days often come right after the worst ones, so trying to time the exits and entries usually costs more than it saves. Missing just the 10 best days of the last few decades has roughly halved long-run returns, and most of those days occurred during bear markets.

Why did the 2020 crash recover so much faster than 2008?

  1. Only tech crashed
  2. It was an external shock to a financial system that wasn't overleveraged, and the policy response was immediate and enormous
  3. Stocks were cheaper
  4. The virus disappeared
Crashes without a broken credit system tend to recover faster.

Which ingredient appears in almost every major bubble?

  1. Government ownership
  2. Easy money and leverage, alongside a compelling story and extrapolation of recent gains
  3. High taxes
  4. Falling prices
